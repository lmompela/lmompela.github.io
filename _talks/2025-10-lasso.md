## Abstract

Ludovic Mompelat & Rose Accilien

In multilingual healthcare environments, machine translation is often called upon to bridge communication gaps, but for underrepresented languages like Haitian Creole, existing tools fall short. Haitian Creole is not a uniform code but a continuum spanning from Kreyòl rèk (basilectal/Creole-like variety) to Kreyòl swa (mesolectal, French-influenced). Yet commercial systems routinely ignore linguistic variation, resulting in mistranslations that compromise both care quality and patient trust. This research is an ongoing project that addresses this gap through a variation-aware evaluation of Haitian Creole medical translations, positioned as a key intermediate step in a broader long-term initiative to develop ethically grounded, AI-assisted translation tools for healthcare.
The methodology builds directly on the framework proposed in prior work, which outlines five core tasks for Haitian Creole NLP in healthcare: 1) modeling linguistic variation, 2) handling code-switching, 3) translating domain-specific terminology, 4) aligning outputs with community norms, and 5) ensuring ethical and practical usability. The present project focuses on the first and fourth tasks by assembling a parallel corpus of English medical source texts and their Haitian Creole translations generated by commercial systems. Each sentence is evaluated using a multidimensional annotation rubric that assesses: 1) linguistic adequacy, 2) fluency, 3) variety alignment (with respect to Kreyòl swa and Kreyòl rèk), and 4) severity or risk level in the medical context. Annotators flag translations that are misleading or inadequate and assign them a risk level—high (e.g., medical terminology errors), medium (e.g., procedural confusion), or low (e.g., non-critical communication). Suggested corrections and error-type tags are provided to support downstream modeling.
These annotations serve as the foundation for benchmarking machine translation performance and support the training of lightweight classifiers used in reinforcement learning pipelines. By emphasizing variety-aware and risk-sensitive revision, this project contributes both to improving translation quality and to ensuring patient safety in linguistically complex environments.
The next stage of this initiative will prioritize collaboration with Haitian Creole speakers, linguists, healthcare professionals, and interpreters to guide data collection, annotation guidelines, and practical deployment. This participatory step is essential to ensuring the resulting system reflects community norms, respects the sociolinguistic diversity of Haitian Creole, and supplements, rather than replaces, the vital work of human translators and interpreters.
This project ultimately advocates for a socially responsible, linguistically inclusive approach to low-resource NLP in healthcare and underscores the importance of modeling not just across languages, but within them. It also offers a replicable path forward for building equitable language technologies that adapt to communities, rather than asking communities to adapt to the technology.
